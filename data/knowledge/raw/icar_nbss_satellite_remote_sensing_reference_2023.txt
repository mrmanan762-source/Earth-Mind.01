1. REMOTE SENSING FUNDAMENTALS
Remote sensing acquires information about the Earth's surface without direct contact,
using sensors on satellites or aircraft that record reflected or emitted electromagnetic
radiation. Passive sensors rely on sunlight; active sensors (for example radar) emit their
own energy.

2. SATELLITE IMAGERY AND SPECTRAL BANDS
Multispectral sensors record reflectance in bands including blue (450-520 nm), green
(520-600 nm), red (630-690 nm), near-infrared (NIR, 750-900 nm) and shortwave infrared
(SWIR). Vegetation strongly reflects NIR and absorbs red light.

3. SPATIAL, TEMPORAL AND SPECTRAL RESOLUTION
- Spatial resolution: ground area represented by one pixel (for example 10 m, 30 m, 250 m).
- Temporal resolution: revisit frequency (for example daily, 5-day, 16-day).
- Spectral resolution: number and width of recorded bands.
Trade-offs exist: finer spatial detail often means lower revisit frequency.

4. VEGETATION INDICES
NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index) = (NIR - Red) / (NIR + Red); values range
from -1 to +1, with healthy vegetation typically 0.3-0.9. EVI (Enhanced Vegetation
Index) reduces atmospheric and soil background effects and is useful in dense canopies.

5. CROP MONITORING AND VEGETATION HEALTH
Time-series vegetation indices support crop area estimation, growth monitoring, stress
detection and yield forecasting. Declining NDVI during a season can indicate drought
stress, pest damage or disease; rising NDVI indicates active growth.

6. LAND COVER AND CHANGE
Multispectral imagery supports land-use/land-cover classification and change detection,
helping monitor cropping patterns, fallows and deforestation.

7. CLOUD COVER AND IMAGE QUALITY
Clouds and shadows obscure the surface and reduce usable observations, particularly in
monsoon seasons. Compositing (for example maximum NDVI composites) reduces cloud
contamination. Image quality depends on sensor calibration, sun angle and atmospheric
conditions.

8. SEASONAL COMPARISON
Comparing vegetation indices across seasons or years (same crop and phenological stage)
helps detect anomalies. Comparisons must control for differences in acquisition date,
sensor and atmospheric conditions.

9. AGRICULTURAL APPLICATIONS
Applications include crop inventory, irrigation scheduling support, drought assessment,
pest and disease scouting, and precision agriculture (variable-rate inputs).

10. LIMITATIONS AND UNCERTAINTY
Vegetation indices are indirect and can saturate in dense canopies; mixed pixels,
atmospheric effects, clouds and geometric errors introduce uncertainty. Interpretation
must combine remote sensing with ground observations. Note: this reference describes
static remote-sensing concepts only. Actual observations, acquisition dates and current
field conditions must come from the live Satellite Intelligence system, not this document.
